Abstract

Objective

Motion artifact in electroencephalography (EEG) recorded during walking is time-locked to the gait cycle, carries information about walking such as gait speed, and cannot be fully removed by conventional preprocessing. Several studies have decoded gait speed with high accuracy from minimally processed EEG, raising the possibility that their decoders relied on motion artifact rather than brain activity. We tested this by examining whether decoding accuracy decreases as more motion artifact is attenuated.

Approach

We used an open-source dataset of 10 young and 10 older adults walking on a treadmill at four speeds (0.25–1.0 m/s), recorded with a dual-layer system that captures scalp EEG and motion artifact through noise electrodes. We compared three preprocessing pipelines: minimal pre-processing, BeMoBIL pre-processing, and iCanClean, which uses the noise electrodes as a reference to attenuate motion artifact. For each subject, we segmented the EEG into windows equal to each subject's longest stride time, then trained a one-dimensional convolutional neural network on one run per speed and tested on the other, for each pipeline and for the noise electrodes alone.

Main results

The noise electrodes alone, which record no brain activity, decoded gait speed with the highest accuracy (0.95 in young and 0.92 in older adults), comparable to minimally processed EEG (0.94 in young, 0.82 in older adults) and BeMoBIL-processed EEG (0.93 in young, 0.89 in older adults). Accuracy fell significantly after iCanClean in both groups (0.51 in young, 0.65 in older adults), with a larger drop in young adults. Two other architectures, EEGNet and EEG-TCNet, reached comparable accuracy on iCanClean-processed EEG.

Significance

These results indicate that gait-speed decoding accuracy decreases as motion artifacts are attenuated, suggesting that the high accuracy reported for minimally processed EEG was driven largely by gait-speed-related features in motion artifacts rather than by cortical activity. More broadly, they suggest that motion artifacts, rather than being discarded, can be leveraged as an informative kinematic signal for studying neuromechanics.

1Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a non-invasive technique for recording cortical activity using electrodes placed on the scalp [1]. It is widely used to study brain activity in clinical and research settings because of its high temporal resolution, low cost, and portability relative to other neuroimaging modalities [2, 3, 4]. For example, Jing et al. trained a deep neural network on clinical EEG recordings to detect interictal epileptiform discharges, a hallmark of epilepsy, with accuracy matching that of expert neurophysiologists [5]. Beyond the clinic, EEG's portability has enabled mobile brain/body imaging (MoBI) studies, in which brain activity is recorded during walking and other dynamic movements [6, 7]. In MoBI studies of walking, Gwin et al. [8] showed that electrocortical activity is coupled to the gait cycle during treadmill walking, and Nordin et al. [9] demonstrated that the spectrum over the sensorimotor cortex varies systematically with gait speed. Together, these findings indicate that scalp EEG recorded during walking contains features that are both phase-locked to the gait cycle and dependent on walking speed.

Building on these gait-related features, several studies have decoded gait kinematics—joint angles, gait phases, and walking speed—directly from EEG. Early work used linear models to reconstruct lower-limb joint angles during treadmill walking from low-frequency EEG [10, 11]. With the development of deep learning, decoding accuracy has improved substantially. For example, Fu et al [12] proposed a hierarchical graph convolutional network (GCN) to regress six lower-limb joint angles from band-pass-filtered EEG. Tortora et al. [13] trained a long short-term memory (LSTM) network to classify swing and stance phases from EEG recorded during walking. Gait speed itself has also been decoded: Korik et al. [14] combined a convolutional neural network (CNN) with an LSTM to decode walking speed from event-related spectral perturbation features, and Katmah et al. [15] trained a two-dimensional CNN on partial directed coherence maps to classify three treadmill speeds. Our previous study also showed that high-pass-filtered EEG data can accurately decode gait speed [16]. Collectively, these results indicate that scalp EEG recorded during walking carries enough gait-related features for deep neural networks to reconstruct kinematics with high fidelity.

However, recent work has indicated that EEG recorded during walking is contaminated by motion artifacts. Kline et al [17] isolated movement artifact by blocking electrophysiological signals with a non-conductive cap and found that its spectrum was structured within the gait cycle and varied with walking speed, subject, and electrode location. Castermans et al. also highlighted the pollution of EEG with gait-related motion artifacts, specifically in the low-delta (∼1 Hz) and high-gamma bands (40-150 Hz) [18]. Critically, the artifact-removal methods commonly applied to mobile EEG attenuate rather than eliminate this contamination. Gwin et al. applied channel-based artifact template regression followed by independent component analysis (ICA) to EEG recorded during walking and running, and reported that during running the procedure reduced but did not eliminate gait-related artifact [19]. Oliveira et al. proposed a correlation-based channel rejection method that likewise attenuated, rather than removed, motion-related artifact during walking [20]. More recently, Downey and Ferris compared iCanClean, a reference-guided method for dual-layer EEG, against other real-time artifact-removal algorithms using a phantom head with simulated brain sources [21]. Although these studies reported successful motion-artifact removal, none verified that the motion artifact was fully removed because they lacked ground-truth cortical activity. However, in studies that decode gait kinematics from EEG data, most merely used a temporal filter to preprocess EEG data before training the decoder, and only a few applied more effective cleaning protocols—channel rejection, ASR, or ICA to attenuate motion artifacts. Therefore, the possibility that the successful decoding of gait kinematics reported by these studies rests partially or entirely on motion artifacts cannot be excluded.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate whether gait-speed decoding from EEG depends on motion artifacts. We used the open-source “Mind in Motion” dataset, in which young and older adults walked on a treadmill at four speeds while a dual-layer EEG system recorded scalp EEG and motion artifacts captured by separate noise electrodes. We trained a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) to decode gait speed from EEG processed with three pipelines: (a) a minimal pre-processing pipeline with high-pass filtering and amplitude-based channel rejection, (b) the preprocessing module of the BeMoBIL pipeline, developed for MoBI EEG studies [22], and (c) iCanClean, which uses the noise and electromyography recordings as references to attenuate movement- and muscle-related artifacts in the EEG pre-processing [21, 23]. We also trained the decoder on the noise electrodes alone, as an artifact-only control. To our knowledge, dual-layer EEG has been used only to characterize and remove motion artifacts [9, 24, 23]; using it here provided a benchmark that no previous EEG gait-decoding study has reported and allowed us to ask what an EEG-based gait-speed decoder actually learns. We hypothesized that decoding accuracy would be highest for the model trained on minimally processed EEG and lowest for the model trained on iCanClean-processed EEG, because minimal processing retains the most gait-speed-related motion artifact, whereas iCanClean uses the noise and electromyography recordings to remove most of it. This work therefore indicates how much of the gait-speed information in walking EEG is carried by motion artifact rather than neural signals, and how EEG-based gait decoding results should be interpreted.

2Method

2.1Description of EEG Dataset

This study utilized the publicly available “Mind in Motion: Young and Older Adults Walking Over Uneven Terrain” dataset by [25], available on the OpenNeuro Platform (ds004625 and ds006095). The dataset included 32 young adults (20-40 years) and 71 older adults (>65 years). However, we selected only 10 participants with the fastest steady walking speed from each age group.

Participants were instructed to walk on a treadmill at four different gait speeds (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 m/s), in randomized order. Each speed condition consisted of two 3-minutes runs, with a 3-minutes seated, eyes-open resting period between each runs [26, 27, 28]. The EEG data were recorded using a custom-built dual-layer EEG system consisting of 120 scalp-facing electrodes to capture scalp EEG signals and 120 noise electrodes to record motion artifacts [29]. In addition to the EEG and noise electrodes, eight electromyography (EMG) channels were recorded from the neck region to capture muscle artifacts from the bilateral sternocleidomastoid and trapezius muscles [24]. All signals (EEG, noise, and EMG) were recorded at a sampling rate of 500 Hz. Furthermore, shoe insole sensors and inertial measurement unit (IMU) sensors captured gait events during walking.

2.2Data Preprocessing Pipelines

We adopted three pipelines to preprocess the EEG data: A) a minimal pre-processing pipeline that retains as many original components of EEG data as possible; B) the BeMoBIL pre-processing pipeline [22] designed to automatically pre-process the MoBI EEG data; and C) the iCanClean pipeline, utilizing the CCA algorithm and other sensors to attenuate the artifacts [21, 23]. In this work, we used the EMG data and noise data recorded from dual-layer EEG to attenuate movement- and muscle-activation-related artifacts [29, 17]. We implemented all three pipelines using EEGLAB [30] in MATLAB.

2.2.1Minimal Pre-processing Pipeline

The minimal pre-processing pipeline applied a high-pass filter with a cut-off frequency of 1 Hz to remove drift from the raw EEG data, a widely used first step in EEG pre-processing [31, 32, 33]. We then inspected each channel of the filtered data for bad channels by comparing its mean absolute amplitude against a fixed threshold of 100 µV, a value typically regarded as abnormally high for scalp EEG [34, 35]. We rejected channels exceeding the threshold and reconstructed them by spherical interpolation from neighboring channels (Figure 1 (A)).

2.2.2BeMoBIL Pre-processing Pipeline

The BeMoBIL pipeline begins by removing line noise with the Zapline-plus method [36], which outperforms the commonly used CleanLine approach on EEG data with nonstationary line noise, such as MoBI recordings [37]. The line-noise-cleaned data were then re-referenced to the common average, and each channel was inspected for bad-channel detection: the correlation coefficient between each channel and a reconstruction of that channel estimated from neighboring channels via random sample consensus (RANSAC) was computed, and channels with a correlation below 0.8—the threshold recommended by the BeMoBIL—were flagged as bad and removed. We iterated this detection 5 times, as we detected no additional bad channels after 5 iterations for all selected subjects. Finally, we interpolated the rejected channels using the same approach as in the minimal pre-processing pipeline, and re-referenced the interpolated data to the common average.

2.2.3iCanClean Pipeline

The implementation of the iCanClean pipeline in this work utilizes the noise electrodes that are placed on top of the scalp EEG channels and EMG electrodes to remove movement- and muscle-activation-related artifacts. We first applied a 1 Hz high-pass filter to the raw data from all channel types (EEG, noise, and EMG) and an additional 20 Hz low-pass filter to the EMG signals. We then re-referenced each channel set to its own common average and removed 60 and 120 Hz line noise from all data. Next, we flagged and removed channels whose mean amplitude deviated by more than 5 standard deviations from their channel set mean, then re-referenced the remaining channels to their common average. We iterated this rejection–re-referencing procedure until no further bad channels were detected, which required at most 3 iterations for all selected subjects. We then applied iCanClean in two stages. In the first stage, we removed movement-related artifacts using the dual-layer noise electrodes as the reference, segmenting the data into 4 s windows and removing EEG components whose canonical correlation with the reference exceeded an R2 threshold of 0.65. In the second stage, we removed muscle-related artifacts using the EMG recordings as the reference, with the same 4 s window length and an R2 threshold of 0.4. We adopted these parameter values from prior studies using the Mind-in-Motion dataset [28, 38] (Figure 1(C)).

2.3One-dimensional Convolutional Neural Network

We designed a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) to decode gait speed from EEG data. The model uses 1D convolutional layers, which have been shown to extract temporal features efficiently from time-series signals such as EEG and EMG [39, 40, 41, 42]. As shown in Figure 2, the model takes a window of EEG data as input and extracts temporal features through five sequentially connected convolution blocks. Each block contains three convolutional layers, and each convolutional layer's output is normalized by batch normalization and then passed through a rectified linear unit (ReLU) nonlinearity. At the end of each block, the resulting feature maps are processed by a max-pooling layer (blocks 1–4 only), which reduces the temporal dimension, and a dropout layer (p = 0.4), which mitigates overfitting [43, 44]. After the convolution blocks, a classifier with two dense layers maps the extracted features to the gait-speed classes.

All convolutional layers use a kernel length of 21 samples, with 10 samples of zero padding on each side of their input (“same” padding). Therefore, each convolution can preserve the temporal length of its input, and only the max-pooling layers reduce temporal resolution. As max pooling halves the temporal resolution after each of the first four blocks, the number of filters doubles from block to block (16 → 32 → 64 → 128 → 256), so that the size of the convolutional feature maps remains constant during convolution, following common CNN design practice [45, 46]. After the final block, adaptive average pooling takes the mean over the temporal axis, collapsing it to a single value per filter. Consequently, no trainable parameter depends on the window length W, and the same architecture accepts input windows of any length. The resulting 256-dimensional feature vector passes through a hidden dense layer (64 units, ReLU) and an output dense layer that produces logits for the four gait-speed classes, trained with cross-entropy loss. The number of convolutional blocks, the number of convolutional layers per block, and the kernel length were selected with the hyperparameter-optimization framework Optuna [47]. The complete model comprises 4,641,714 trainable parameters.

2.4Training and Validation of the CNN Model

We used a sliding-window method with designated window length (W) and step length (S) to segment the preprocessed EEG data into windows for training and testing the CNN model (Figure 2). We set the window length to the stride time, defined as the average time between consecutive right heel strikes at a given gait speed. Specifically, we used each subject's stride time at 0.25 m/s, since this speed has the longest stride time; therefore, a full gait cycle of EEG data can be extracted for all gait speeds. Because stride time varies across individuals, the window length therefore differed between subjects. Consecutive windows were gapped by a step of 5 samples (10 ms at the 500 Hz sampling rate). We chose this step size for two reasons: first, it corresponds to a prediction rate of 100 Hz, which is sufficient for real-time implementation; second, the large overlap between windows substantially increases the volume of training data available to the CNN.

We implemented a rolling-window approach designed for time-series data [48] to partition the EEG data into training and validation sets. Unlike the widely adopted sample-based k-fold cross-validation, the rolling-window approach always trains the CNN on a contiguous past segment of a run and validates it on the segment that immediately follows. We used a three-fold rolling-window cross-validation scheme with two repetitions to train, validate, and test the CNN on each subject's EEG data. In each fold n, the training window started at (n-1) × 18 s and had a fixed length of 70% of a run (126 s of the 180 s run); we used the subsequent 10% (18 s) of the run to validate the model during training. After training, we used the entire remaining run (180 s) to test the trained model (Figure 3). Because each run in the dataset was recorded independently, this testing scheme evaluates the model's inter-session generalizability. We repeated the rolling-window cross-validation twice, swapping the roles of the two runs so each run served once for training and validation and once for testing. We performed the partitioning before segmentation to ensure no window spans the boundary between the two sets: within each fold, we first split the EEG data into training and validation sets as described above, and then independently segmented each set into input windows using a sliding window with overlap.

We implemented, trained, and evaluated all CNN models in Python using the PyTorch deep learning framework. We used cross-entropy to evaluate the model during training, and the Adaptive Gradient Descent Optimizer with a 0.0001 learning rate to train the model. For all deep CNNs, training was performed for up to 100 epochs. During training, we monitored the validation loss and halved the learning rate at the epoch when the validation loss failed to decrease for 10 consecutive epochs. We also applied early stopping from the 21st epoch; it terminated the training when the change in validation loss remained below 0.001 for 20 consecutive epochs. All training and testing are done on a workstation with an RTX Pro 6000 dedicated GPU.

2.5Performance metrics and statistical analysis

Decoding performance was quantified as classification accuracy: the number of correctly classified test windows divided by the total number of test windows in a subject’s held-out test set. Because each subject’s model was trained and evaluated six times (two repetitions × three runs per repetition), the six accuracy values were averaged, yielding one accuracy per subject per pipeline. These per-subject values were used in the statistical analysis. With four balanced speed classes, chance-level accuracy was 25%.

The effects of preprocessing pipeline and age group on decoding accuracy were analyzed with a linear mixed-effects model (LMM), with the per-subject accuracy described above as the dependent variable. Pipeline (Minimal Pre-process, BeMoBIL Pre-process, iCanClean) was a within-subject fixed factor, age group (young, old) a between-subject fixed factor, and their interaction was included; a random intercept for subject accounted for the repeated measurements across pipelines (Accuracy ∼ Age Group × Pipeline + (1 | subject)). The noise-electrode condition served as a reference level for interpretation and was not entered in the model.

The significance of each fixed effect and their interaction was assessed using a two-way mixed ANOVA on the fitted model (lmerTest). If statistical significance was determined for the interaction between fixed effects, we would compute Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means (emmeans::emmeans) for the interaction effects. All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.3), and we used p < 0.05 as the significance level for all tests.

3Results

3.1Impact of the Preprocessing Pipeline on the EEG Data

Table 1 lists the number of EEG channels rejected by each pipeline. The minimal pre-processing pipeline rejected the fewest channels, the BeMoBIL pipeline rejected more, and the iCanClean pipeline rejected the most, rejecting more channels than BeMoBIL for most young and older adults' EEG data.

Figures 4 and 5 show stride-locked ERP images and average waveforms at Cz after each preprocessing pipeline, together with the overlying noise electrode (N-Cz), for a representative young and older adult. All ERPs are plotted with strides time-warped to the gait cycle (0–100%, right heel strike to right heel strike) and the dashed lines marking left toe-off (LTO), left heel strike (LHS), and right toe-off (RTO). In both subjects, the minimally pre-processed EEG showed a clear stride-locked pattern, visible as vertical bands that were consistent across strides and as reproducible deflections in the average waveform. The BeMoBIL pipeline left this pattern essentially unchanged. In contrast, the iCanClean pipeline removed most of it: the vertical bands largely disappeared from the ERP images, and the average waveform fell to roughly ±1 µV at all speeds.

The stride-locked pattern was speed-dependent in both the EEG and the noise electrodes. In the minimally pre-processed and BeMoBIL-processed EEG, it was the strongest and most consistent at 1 m/s and weak at 0.25 m/s in both subjects. At N-Cz, both young and older adults showed a strong stride-locked pattern at the three faster speeds that significantly diminished at 0.25 m/s.

3.2Decoding Accuracy of the CNN Model with EEG Data Processed by Different Pipelines

Figure 6 shows the group-level and per-subject test accuracies of the proposed 1D-CNN trained on the noise-electrode signals and on the EEG data from each of the three preprocessing pipelines. The two-way ANOVA on the fitted linear mixed model revealed a significant main effect of preprocessing pipeline and a significant interaction effect between pipeline and age group (both p<0.001), whereas the main effect of age group was not significant. Moreover, the motion artifact measured by noise electrodes alone yielded comparable accuracy, indicating that gait speed can be decoded from motion artifact without any cortical contributions.

Figures 7 and 8 show the group-level confusion matrices and the per-class recall for each pipeline (the percentage value in the diagonal of each confusion matrix). With minimally pre-processed and BeMoBIL-processed EEG, recall exceeded 80% for nearly every speed, and the few errors were concentrated between adjacent speeds. After being processed by iCanClean, recall fell to 54–62% in young adults and 56–72% in older adults, and the errors were no longer confined to neighboring speeds, indicating that the speed-dependent structure exploited by the classifier had been largely removed rather than merely blurred.

3.3Decoding Accuracy of Alternative Deep-Learning Architectures on iCanClean-Processed EEG

The EEG data preprocessed by the iCanClean pipeline achieved the same accuracy when trained by the EEGNet and EEG-TCNet (Figure 9). All three architectures decoded gait speed significantly below the accuracies obtained from minimally pre-processed or BeMoBIL-processed EEG, and their accuracies were close to those of the 1DCNN. The post-cleaning drop in accuracy was therefore not attributable to the choice of network architecture.

4Discussion

This study investigated whether motion artifacts facilitate high-accuracy gait-speed decoding from mobile EEG. We hypothesized that decoding accuracy would decline as more motion artifact was attenuated, and the results supported this hypothesis. Our results indicated the CNN accuracy fell sharply when using EEG data with motion artifact mostly attenuated by the iCanClean pipeline. However, the noise electrodes alone, which recorded no cortical activity, achieved comparably high accuracy as the minimally pre-processed and BeMoBIL pre-processed EEG data. This decrease was reproduced by two other network architectures; therefore, it suggests that the pre-processing pipeline, rather than the decoder, could affect the CNN accuracy.

Our results suggest that gait-speed-related features of the motion artifact, rather than cortical activity, largely drove the high decoding accuracies. CNN accuracy dropped sharply for iCanClean-processed EEG, and the stride-locked ERP pattern disappeared with it, whereas the same pattern persisted in the minimally processed and BeMoBIL-processed EEG and also appeared in the noise-electrode data. This is consistent with Kline et al [17], who showed that motion artifact isolated from EEG varies with gait speed. The CNN accuracy with iCanClean-processed EEG is comparable to a previous study that trained a 2D-CNN model to decode treadmill walking speed from the EEG data with motion artifact removed by the ICA method. [15]. Although the two studies differ in the number of gait-speed classes and the validation scheme, both suggest that gait-speed decoding accuracy is modest once motion artifacts have been attenuated. Nonetheless, iCanClean-processed EEG still yielded roughly twice the baseline (25%), which could reflect residual motion artifacts, since iCanClean attenuates rather than removes them [23], or gait-speed-related features in cortical activity that CNN models can learn [9] but have less support in accurate decoding of gait-speed than motion artifacts.

The number of rejected channels does not explain the difference in decoding accuracy between pipelines. The BeMoBIL pipeline rejected more channels than the minimal pre-processing pipeline (Table 1), yet it did not reduce decoding accuracy relative to minimal pre-processing. However, the iCanClean pipeline rejected the most channels and achieved the worst CNN accuracy. Together, these results indicate that the channel-rejection stage attenuated far fewer gait-speed-related motion artifacts than the CCA-based motion-artifact attenuation algorithm using noise-electrode data. This outcome follows from the purpose of the channel rejection criteria. The amplitude-based criteria of the minimal pre-processing pipelines are intended to remove poorly connected channels rather than motion artifacts [21]. BeMoBIL's channel-rejection criteria follow the PREP pipeline [33], which flags channels that are poorly correlated with other channels. These criteria are designed to detect recording failures such as broken electrodes and poor contact. An artifact shared across neighboring electrodes, such as a stride-locked artifact from cable sway and cap movement, increases rather than decreases a channel's agreement with other channels and therefore passes these criteria. This does not rule out channel rejection as a means of attenuating motion artifact, but it requires criteria designed for that purpose. Oliveira et al. [20] proposed the template correlation rejection (TCR) algorithm, which uses recorded gait events to identify channels whose stride-by-stride waveform consistently correlates with a stride-locked template, and showed that it identifies and removes EEG channels contaminated by motion artifact. Without such criteria, temporal filtering and conventional channel rejection leave the gait-speed-related features of the motion artifact largely intact and exploitable by a gait-speed decoder.

The findings show that motion artifacts could significantly facilitate high decoding accuracy of gait speed from EEG data. Recently, studies have accurately decoded not only gait speed but also other gait kinematics, such as joint angles and gait phases, from EEG data with only minimal processing using temporal filters and deep learning models [14, 49, 12]. We assume the high decoding accuracies reported in these works could be facilitated by the motion artifacts. However, further studies are needed to validate this assumption, as this work only investigates the contribution of motion artifacts to decoding gait speed from EEG data. Moreover, this finding highlights the practical implications of motion artifacts: rather than treating them as noise and removing them [50, 19, 51, 29], the gait-speed-related features they carry can serve as a complementary signal for decoding gait speed during walking MoBI studies.

Two limitations bound the scope of these conclusions. First, the EEG data used in this study were recorded during treadmill walking at speeds up to 1 m/s. Motion artifacts likely contribute more strongly at faster speeds and during overground walking, so the decoding results reported here should be extended to these more naturalistic conditions in future studies. Second, we decoded gait speed from processed EEG data and did not isolate cortical sources with ICA methods such as AMICA [52]. Whether gait speed can be decoded from cortical activity alone, once motion and muscle components are separated, therefore remains an open question for future work.

5Conclusion

This study shows that motion artifacts facilitate the high-accuracy gait-speed decoding from mobile EEG data. A CNN decoder was trained with noise data recorded by noise electrodes, which have no cortical activity, and EEG data processed by minimal pre-processing, BeMoBIL pre-processing, and the iCanClean pipeline, with motion artifacts attenuated gradually. CNN accuracy dropped sharply with iCanClean-processed EEG data but remained high with minimal pre-processing and BeMoBIL-preprocessed EEG and noise data. This accuracy drop also reproduced across different CNN architectures. Moreover, the stride-locked pattern was also lost in the ERP image generated from iCanClean-processed EEG, while it remained in the one generated from EEG preprocessed by other pipelines and noise data. These results support our finding. Future work should test whether these conclusions extend to overground walking and faster speeds, and whether gait speed remains decipherable from cortical sources isolated by ICA.
